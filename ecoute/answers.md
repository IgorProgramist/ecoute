Q: How do you communicate technical trade-offs to non-technical teammates?
A: I avoid starting with engine terminology. I explain the consequence in product terms: visual quality, loading time, memory, risk, implementation time, or maintainability. Then I give the options and what each one costs. For example, instead of saying “this breaks batching,” I might say, “this version gives us the visual effect, but it adds extra rendering cost on every copy; this alternative is slightly simpler visually but safer for mobile performance.”

Q: How do you step into an existing pipeline and become productive?
A: I start by observing before changing things. I look at how prefabs, folders, naming, Addressables, UI, animation, Git workflow, and existing features are structured. I find one or two similar examples that are already accepted in production and use them as a reference. Once I understand the conventions, I can work much faster without inventing a parallel pipeline that the team then has to maintain.

Q: How do you investigate a visual or technical issue independently?
A: I reproduce the issue first and define exactly what is wrong. Then I narrow the problem by layers: source asset, import settings, prefab or Canvas setup, animation, material or rendering, runtime state, loading, or code. I test one hypothesis at a time and use profiler or debugger evidence when performance is involved. I try to finish with both the fix and an understanding of the root cause.

Q: What does root-cause analysis mean to you?
A: It means not stopping at the first change that makes the symptom disappear. If a prefab is wrong because one value was manually changed, I want to know why that value became wrong and whether other prefabs can have the same issue. A good fix solves the current problem and, when reasonable, reduces the chance of the same class of problem coming back.

Q: How do you balance visual quality and performance?
A: I first identify which visual parts are important to the player and which technical constraint is actually limiting us. Then I look for the cheapest way to keep the important visual result. Sometimes the answer is a smaller texture, sometimes a different effect, different loading timing, reusing content, or simplifying something that the player barely notices. I prefer evidence and comparison over “quality at any cost” or “optimize everything.”

Q: How do you deliver within a release timeline when the feature is still changing?
A: I separate must-have work from polish and risk. I try to identify the decisions that can cause expensive rework early, keep the implementation modular where possible, and communicate the impact of changes quickly. If the timeline gets tight, I’d rather agree on a smaller stable version than silently keep adding complexity and miss the release.

Q: How do you prioritize when several things are urgent?
A: I look at release impact, blockers for other people, production risk, and how expensive it will be to delay each item. I clarify the priority if it isn’t obvious, then focus on the highest-impact task instead of switching constantly. I also communicate early when two “urgent” tasks cannot realistically be finished at the same time.

Q: When would you build an editor tool instead of doing the task manually?

If it happens every week across many assets and people keep making the same mistakes, automation becomes much more valuable. I also want the tool to be simple enough that maintaining it does not become a bigger problem than the workflow it replaced.

The goal is polished production quality, not endless polishing with no product value.
